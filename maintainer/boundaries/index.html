Setting Boundaries for Open Source Maintainers

This guide helps maintainers establish and maintain healthy boundaries.

---Thank you to all the maintainers who shared their insights for this guide!

Why Boundaries Matter

Boundaries are the foundation of sustainable open source work. They protect your time, energy, and well-being while setting clear expectations for your community.

Without boundaries, you risk: - Burnout from overcommitting - Resentment from unmet expectations - Loss of work-life balance - Diminished enjoyment of the work

With boundaries, you create: - Predictability for yourself and others - Space for focused, meaningful work - Protection against scope creep and unreasonable demands - Permission to prioritize your well-being

Types of Boundaries

1. Time Boundaries

What they cover: When you work, how long, and how often.

Examples: - "I work on Surypus weekdays from 9am to 5pm EST." - "I don't work on weekends." - "I take one week off per quarter."

How to set them: 1. Decide your available hours 2. Communicate them clearly 3. Stick to them consistently 4. Update if your situation changes

Template:

"I am available to work on Surypus on [days] from [time] to [time] [timezone]. I don't work weekends. I will try to respond to issues within [timeframe]."

2. Scope Boundaries

What they cover: What types of work you accept and what you decline.

Examples: - "I don't review PRs that change the public API without prior discussion." - "I won't work on feature requests without an approved issue." - "I focus on core functionality, not plugins or integrations."

How to set them: 1. Define your scope clearly 2. Document it in CONTRIBUTING or a dedicated document 3. Politely decline out-of-scope requests with explanation 4. Offer alternatives when possible

Template:

"Thanks for the suggestion! This is outside the scope of Surypus. The project focuses on [scope]. You might want to [alternative suggestion] or consider [alternative project]."

3. Communication Boundaries

What they cover: How you communicate, through which channels, and with what response expectations.

Examples: - "I respond to issues within 48 hours on weekdays." - "I don't check email evenings or weekends." - "I prefer discussing technical decisions in issues, not DMs."

How to set them: 1. State your communication preferences clearly 2. Use templates for common responses 3. Set up auto-responders for breaks 4. Mute notifications outside work hours 5. Redirect private conversations to public channels

Template:

"I check issues weekly and respond within 48 hours on weekdays. For urgent matters, please cc [backup maintainer]. I prefer discussing technical decisions in public issues so others can learn."

4. Emotional Boundaries

What they cover: How you handle criticism, conflict, and emotional demands.

Examples: - "I don't read every comment on every issue." - "I separate feedback on my code from feedback on me as a person." - "I don't feel responsible for fixing every user's problem."

How to set them: 1. Recognize emotional triggers 2. Develop healthy responses to criticism 3. Take breaks when emotions run high 4. Seek support from other maintainers 5. Remember: you're not responsible for everyone's happiness

Common Boundary Challenges

"But it's open source—I should be available!"

Response: Open source doesn't mean unlimited availability. You choose your level of involvement. Many successful maintainers have clear boundaries.

"Users will be upset if I say no."

Response: Clear boundaries actually reduce frustration. Users may be temporarily disappointed, but consistent boundaries build trust over time.

"I feel guilty saying no."

Response: Guilt is common but not a reliable guide. Remind yourself: saying no to one thing means saying yes to your well-being and the things that matter most.

"I'm afraid of losing contributors."

Response: Clear boundaries often attract better contributors—those who respect your time and share your values.

Templates for Common Scenarios

Declining a Feature Request

"Thanks for the suggestion! This is outside the scope of Surypus, which focuses on [scope]. This might be better suited for [alternative project] or a plugin. If you'd like to explore this further, please open an issue to discuss."

Responding to Urgent Requests

"I understand this is urgent for you. I'm not available evenings/weekends, but [backup maintainer] might be able to help. Otherwise, I'll look at this during my next available time on [day/time]."

Handling Unreasonable Demands

"I'm not able to [demand]. This is outside the scope of my involvement with Surypus. If you'd like to contribute this yourself, I'm happy to review your PR."

Taking a Break

"I'm taking a break from Surypus maintenance from [start date] to [end date]. I won't be responding to issues or PRs during this time. [Backup maintainer] may be able to help with urgent matters. Thank you for your understanding!"

A Note on Saying No

Saying "no" is a skill. It gets easier with practice.

Why saying no is okay: - You have limited time and energy - Not every request deserves a yes - Saying no protects your ability to say yes to what matters - Clear "no"s are kinder than vague deferrals

How to say no well: 1. Be direct and clear 2. Explain briefly (no over-apologizing) 3. Offer alternatives when possible 4. Do it kindly but firmly

Remember: "No" is a complete sentence. You don't owe lengthy explanations.

Maintaining Your Boundaries

Setting boundaries is one thing. Keeping them is another.

Tips for sticking to boundaries: - Automate reminders: Use calendar alerts for breaks - Use templates: Save pre-written responses for common scenarios - Find accountability: Check in with another maintainer about boundary adherence - Review regularly: Are your boundaries still working? Adjust as needed.

Conclusion

Boundaries aren't barriers—they're guardrails that let you sustainably contribute to open source. They protect your time, energy, and well-being while setting clear expectations for your community.

Start small. Pick one boundary to set this week. Notice how it feels. Adjust as needed.

You deserve to enjoy your open source work without burning out.
